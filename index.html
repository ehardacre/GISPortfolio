The problem · 1 of 2

Summer is the hard season

Southern California summers get hot, and trail work is hard physical labor. As the Pacific Crest Trail Association's regional stewardship coordinator for 510 miles of trail, I had to cancel multiple volunteer projects over heat concerns.

My limit: no volunteer crew out above 90 °F, or above 85 °F without adequate shade.

The problem · 2 of 2

Plans lock in two months out

Agency approvals, crew logistics, and volunteer recruitment add up to about two months. A late-August project gets locked in by late June, long before any forecast can say what that week will bring.

Which stretches of trail are most likely to have safe working weather in late summer?

Answering that ahead of time lets project proposals and work plans for the year be locked in early.

The data

Weather stations

The analysis starts from point measurements: daily maximum temperatures from two station networks. GHCN-Daily stations cluster around towns and valleys. RAWS fire-weather stations reach into remote wildland terrain, closer to where the trail runs.

Each station's 2011–2026 record was averaged into a single number, its typical August–September high, the value every method in this project works from.

The data

One number per station

Colored by average August–September high, the stations start to show the pattern: the hottest readings sit inland and toward the desert, the coolest along the coast and up high.

But it's still just scattered points. The trail runs through the gaps between them, and that's where the estimating has to happen.

Methods · The model

Elevation and the coast

EBK Regression Prediction combines kriging with a regression on two predictors mapped across the whole region: elevation, because air cools with height, and distance to the coast, because the marine layer keeps coastal lowlands cool while inland basins bake.

Neither works alone. Across all stations, elevation barely predicts the late-summer high, because cool coastal lowlands and hot desert floors sit at similar heights. Together, the two explain the pattern between stations.

Methods · Accuracy

The error drops 44%

In cross-validation, which predicts each station from all the others, its root-mean-square error (RMSE) was 2.6 °F, 44% below IDW's 4.7 °F.

The result

The late-summer heat map

The final EBK Regression surface predicts the average August–September high for every point in the region, including every mile of trail.

The trail's coolest ground is in the high country of the San Gabriel, San Bernardino and San Jacinto mountains.

The answer

The trail, mile by mile

Sampled along the centerline, the surface becomes a planning tool: every mile from the Southern Terminus to mile 550, with the road crossings crews use to reach the work.

The coolest late-summer stretches run through the San Jacinto high country, past Onyx Summit in the San Bernardinos, and along Highway 2 in the San Gabriels. The hottest sit at the low crossings: Scissors Crossing, I-10, and the miles around Agua Dulce and Pine Canyon Road.

On the ground

From the map to the trail

The point of all this is the people doing the work. With a year-round picture of late-summer heat, project proposals and work plans can be locked in early, with volunteer crews scheduled on the stretches where the weather is most likely to cooperate.
